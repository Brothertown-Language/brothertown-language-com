*The Brothertown Language Project needs words for the teaching materials it is building, and the historical record holds no community language to take them from — how it derives them by selection among recorded forms, and why the derivation lives in public, under review.*

The Brothertown Language Project is building teaching materials, a starter vocabulary, and a written form for the words — an independent, community-backed effort, not a program of the Brothertown Indian Nation. A project's words have to come from someplace, and for most languages that someplace is a recorded community language. For Brothertown there is none — the historical record speaks English from the community's founding. What the record does hold is the founders' own languages, written down from the seventeenth through the nineteenth centuries. The project derives its words from those records.

The derivation is being written up now because it has a first complete statement: the laws, the weights, the worked forms, and an outward check against modern Mohegan — a revival built from its own records decades earlier. It is published the way reclamation projects in this field have learned to publish reconstruction work: candidate rules marked hypothetical, weights marked provisional, produced forms marked produced, the whole package named for the project's lead linguist rather than settled privately and delivered as settled.

<!-- more -->

And it shapes the project's approach from the ground up. Every word the materials use is either recorded in a founder language or generated under published rules — never invented, never averaged between communities — and every generated word keeps its status visibly where it appears. Under the published weights, the contested choices between the founders' languages resolve to the founders' largest and best-recorded side — the language family modern Mohegan belongs to. What remains open — the rules' validity, the weights, which words to carry for meanings where the vocabularies diverged — is named for the linguist to decide, not papered over.


## The founders and the record

In the eighteenth century, Native communities from what are now Connecticut, Rhode Island, and eastern Long Island — under the leadership of the minister Samson Occom — joined together and founded a new shared community: Brothertown. Their languages were close relatives — siblings rather than strangers — but not identical: different communities said certain words differently.

English took over from the founding itself: the documents created by and about the community speak English from the start. Nothing in the record shows whether Brothertown ever developed its own blended way of speaking the ancestral languages. The one direct list of "Brothertown words" anyone ever collected, in 1904, turned out — by its own collectors' description — to be garbled Ojibwe, picked up from neighbors in Wisconsin, not evidence of the founders' languages.

## How the words are derived and checked

The founders' languages themselves were written down, densely in some cases and thinly in others. They agree on almost everything; in the small, explicit set of places where they differ, history recorded what *each* community actually said. So describing the blended language requires no invention: at each difference, the reconstruction *chooses* one of the recorded forms, and written rules decide which choice wins — the side whose people and records together carry more weight. Where the rules run out and the evidence could still genuinely go either way, the item is flagged for the project's linguist rather than settled by a coin-flip. And the method refuses, categorically, to average — to mix a bit of each community's version into a third form that no community ever said.

Because nothing is invented, the result can be checked against an outcome that arose independently: the modern Mohegan revival, a community that rebuilt its language from its own historical records decades before this project began. Set side by side word by word, 27 of the 52 comparable words come out exactly the same or differ only in ways the reconstruction itself predicts; seven come out letter-for-letter identical. The words that differ mostly differ because the two vocabularies simply ended up keeping different words for a meaning — a fact about the records, not a failure of the method. Every word the reconstruction *produced* rather than *recorded* is visibly marked as produced everywhere it appears, and the weighting rules themselves are provisional, pending the project linguist's review.

## What a koine means for Brothertown

A koine is what a language becomes when speakers of related dialects build one community voice out of several. The Brothertown founders — Samson Occom's circle, whose planning meeting at Mohegan in March 1773 and formal founding of November 7, 1785 united communities from Connecticut, Rhode Island, and Long Island — brought together speakers of y-dialect, n-dialect, and r-dialect Southern New England Algonquian: language communities named for the sound each gave a historical *r* — *y* in Mohegan-Pequot-Montauk-Niantic, *n* in much of the Narragansett record, *r* retained in Tunxis (Quiripi-related).

English took over from the founding. But the founders' languages themselves left dense records — Williams's 1643 *A Key into the Language of America*, Fielding's Mohegan materials, Rudes's 1997 reconstruction of Quiripi phonology, and more — and the comparative history of those languages is published and well understood. The koine claim: the community language Brothertown might have had can be derived from those records on stated, reviewable rules, and the result can be tested against the one related modern revival we have: today's Mohegan.

## The evidence

### 1. Who stood at the founding

Everything rests on knowing which communities contributed Brothertown's people, because the koine's inputs are exactly their languages. The tribe's own history names seven founding groups: Mohegan, the two Pequot towns, Narragansett, Niantic, Montauk, and Tunxis. Two caveats are recorded rather than resolved: some accounts add the Wangunk of the Connecticut River valley as an eighth founding group, and the Stockbridge — a separate community on a separate adjacent Oneida tract — are excluded as a constituent while remaining an external calibration point.

The dialect map of that composition is what matters for the derivation: the Mohegan-Pequot-Montauk-Niantic bloc speaks y-dialect languages; Williams's Narragansett data is itself an n/y mixture; Tunxis speaks a Quiripi-related r-dialect language. Three selection-profiles, one community.

### 2. No recorded Brothertown language

If a distinct Brothertown community language existed in the record, this whole project would be corpus work — read the record, recover the language, as the Wôpanâak project did with its documents. But the record says otherwise. The community is documented as English-speaking from its founding. The single direct vocabulary trace — the "Brothertown words" in Prince and Speck's 1904 Mohegan-Pequot glossary, collected from an old man at Mohegan who had lived at Brothertown, Wisconsin — is, as Prince and Speck themselves describe it, "merely corruptions of Ojibwe forms" (their ⟨Bosu⟩ 'good-day' is the Ojibwe greeting *boozhoo*: the Brothertown community had picked it up). Those ~12 items are unusable as evidence of a Southern New England Algonquian community language.

Even Occom's recorded native name for the settlement, *Eeyamquittoowauconnuck*, is — as presented in the record — a named coinage for the community, not evidence of a distinct community variety. So the koine, if it can be described at all, must be *derived* from the founders' languages — which makes both the method and the marking discipline the real subject of the work.

### 3. The shared spine: the sound history every founder's language inherits

The derivation does not start from zero, and it does not touch the published comparative history. Southern New England Algonquian languages share a descent chain — Proto-Algonquian (PA) → Proto-Eastern Algonquian (PEA) → Proto-Southern New England Algonquian (PSNEA) — carried by Goddard's published laws for the first two legs (a sound *law* is a stated, regular change the comparative record shows happening across a family) and by Costa's 2007 synthesis of the three SNEA-defining changes and the boundary changes to the individual languages. All of that is shared bedrock: the koine inherits *everything* through the PSNEA stage unchanged, and its candidate laws make no exception to, and no bypass of, any attested chain.

The koine's only *new* decisions come at the daughter level, where the founders' languages split from each other; everything before that split is somebody's attested history, not an invention of ours.

### 4. The founders' records

At the daughter level the founding languages differ in exactly a handful of places — which is itself a finding. The comparative record gives, per selection point, one attested reflex per founder (a *reflex* is where an inherited sound landed in a given language): *r* becomes *y*, *n*, or *r* (above); the laryngeal cluster yields *hš* in nearly every founder; word-final *r* yields *š* everywhere it is on the table; syncope follows a Mohegan-Pequot pattern in one taxonomy and a Wampanoag pattern in another. The donor law tables anchor these — Fielding 2005's ordered 39-law list and segment chart for Mohegan-Pequot (as corrected by the project's linguist), Rudes 1997's Quiripi phonology as the second principal donor table, Williams 1643 as the densest conversational record — and the densities differ by orders of magnitude: hundreds of attested Mohegan-Pequot forms against a near-zero Tunxis record.

The density spread is the reason a weighting is needed at all, and the reason an equal weighting is already ruled out: hundreds of attested words do not "count equally" against a near-zero record.

### 5. The weights: demographic × evidential

Two weightings govern the derivation, and they are kept separate on purpose. The *demographic* column asks who was at Brothertown; it is grounded in the mined print record — Love's 1899 Family History appendix (39 founding families, each with a tribal attribution: Narragansett 14, Pequot 8, Tunxis 7, Mohegan 4, Montauk 3, Niantic 2, plus one shared attribution) and the surviving list of the 1773 first-settler band (58 able working men: Narragansett 20, Montauk 13, Mohegan 10, "Farmington" 10, Niantic 5, with the Pequot towns named but deferred). Two independent measurements agree on the ranking: Narragansett the largest single community (~36% of families), the y-dialect bloc the largest aggregate (~44%), Tunxis a real minority (~18%).

The *evidential* column asks what each record can support: roughly 55% y-dialect, 30% Narragansett, 5% Tunxis — plus about 10% for the Wampanoag-area emigrants who joined around the 1850s, after formation, and so carry attestation but no founding demography. Combining the columns puts the y-dialect side at roughly 0.24 against Narragansett at roughly 0.11 — a margin of about 2.2×, and (because the evidential column dominates the product) a margin that cannot flip on small shifts in the demographic figures.

Status of both columns: provisional, print-sourced, gap-marked where the record is quiet (the population share table sits pending the linguist's adjudication, and the known census caveats are carried with it).

### 6. Selection, not averaging: the candidate laws and the tie-breaker ladder

The koine principle: *phonetics are not averaged; for each selection point the community outcome is selected among the attested daughter reflexes.* No sound is invented, no form is blended between founders, and no law contradicts the attested chain. What the weights buy is a deterministic outcome at every point where the founders' languages disagree.

Seven candidate laws (B1–B7) carry the daughter-level decisions — all marked hypothetical, each grounded in an attested reflex, none bypassing the chain:

1. **Non-final *r* → *y*** (the y-dialect reflex, selected automatically by weighted majority; Narragansett's *n* records as the displaced alternant, not an output).
2. **Word-final *r* → *š***, with *s* as a minority variant from the late Wampanoag-side emigrants.
3. **The *hš* cluster surfaces as *hš*** — the reflex of every founding bloc (the Unquachog-type *hs* belongs to the later Wampanoag-area emigrants, outside the founding set).
4. ***k*-palatalization retained** in its simple-palatal (y-dialect) realization.
5. **Syncope follows the Mohegan-Pequot patterns** (the densest attestation).
6. **The intrusive nasal and preaspiration carry over unchanged** — every founder has them, so no selection arises.
7. **The corrected labial and nasal-loss rows carry over as corrected**, applied to the dominant Mohegan-Pequot component.

Where a selection point still has two attested contenders, a four-step tie-breaker ladder decides it in order: weighted majority; then attestation density; then conservative retention (at a true tie, keep the un-innovated PSNEA value — the older side, per the contact-zone finding that neologisms replace older forms on the innovating side); and if that fails, the row is marked *contested* and referred to the linguist. No coin-flips, no averaging — under the computed weights, the first step resolves every contested row, and they resolve y.

### 7. The worked forms — and the counterfactual published alongside

The worked chain produces concrete demonstration forms in the project's practical orthography (Costa letters with BL's values): teeth → ⟨wipitay⟩ (with ⟨wipitan⟩ the displaced Narragansett-side alternant), person → ⟨uyuniw⟩, house → ⟨wiciwôh⟩, chief/sachem → ⟨sôcimôw⟩, heart → ⟨mutáh⟩, three → ⟨nuhshwi⟩, island → ⟨munahan⟩, and the inanimate plural suffix → ⟨-ash⟩.

Alongside the weighted outcome, the derivation publishes its equal-weight counterfactual — what you would get if every founding bloc counted the same: the sound laws themselves would not change, but every selection point with two attested reflexes would become contested, and the koine would come out doublet-riddled rather than y-shaped. Recording the counterfactual is deliberate: it shows exactly what the weights are doing (deciding selections, not changing laws) — disagreeing with the weights never costs the laws.

### 8. The check against modern Mohegan

The generated BL headwords were compared, word by word, against the modern Mohegan revival's own learner's dictionary — read *only* as a comparison target; the modern dictionary is never an input to generation, which keeps the check honest. Each pair received one of five defined verdicts (a fifth, synonym-split, was retired when PA-chain scrutiny re-unified or re-classified its candidates; no row carries it). Of the 55 rows, 7 are letter-for-letter **identical**, 20 are **regular** (same etymon; the differences trace to patterns the paper already records — lost final *n* from *nin/ni* and *kin/ki*, syncope in *munahan/munhan*, the *miy-* give paradigm), 24 are **divergent** (different etymon or formation — different words kept by the two lineages, analyzed per pair), 1 is a systematic inflectional divergence, and 3 have no counterpart in the partial comparison list.

Among the seven letter-for-letter identities, six are clean calibration anchors — ⟨awán⟩ 'who', ⟨nuks⟩ 'yes', ⟨nitôp⟩ 'friend', ⟨nohsh⟩ 'my father', ⟨yo⟩ 'this', ⟨mutáh⟩ 'heart' — where the two lineages agree, a Williams-anchored derivation and a living revival produce the same word; the seventh, ⟨nimat⟩ 'my brother', is letter-identical with a recorded kinship-sense difference (the modern chart glosses 'my older brother'). 27 of the 52 comparable words land identical or predictably regular; the divergent remainder are not noise but information — mostly different attested words held by the two vocabularies, a selection history that belongs to whoever adjudicates the lexicon, not to the sound laws.

## How other reclamation projects do it

Nothing in this method is unprecedented; each piece has working precedent in other projects' public records. That matters, because the precedents discipline us.

The Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project rebuilt Wôpanâak for a community that had been without speakers for over a century and a half from documentary sources — the Eliot Indian Bible plus deeds, wills, and petitions in the language — combining careful study of the texts with "sound change comparisons with other sister languages… to ensure a conservative linguistic foundation" (the project's own description). That is precisely the spine-plus-sisters structure above, minus the koine problem, because Wôpanâak's documentary record is a corpus of attested text rather than a gap.

The Mutsun dictionary prints newly formed words under a heading — "New word made from… we have included them in the dictionary… so that you can tell they were not part of how Mutsun was spoken in the past" — and flags uncertain attestation right in the entry ("[Attested only once]"). The revived Kaurna dictionary adds newly coined modern words formed by the language's own word-formation processes in consultation with elders, and the coinage is openly acknowledged, never hidden (the new-word count is reported differently by source — the Australian Broadcasting Corporation describes some 4,000 new words; the foundation's own materials describe 400 new-and-repurposed entries; the practice, not the figure, is the point). This project's equivalent is the star discipline: every generated BL form is marked generated, never attested, and every form carries its generation path (attestation-derived, chain-derived, generated-systematic, or provisional).

The palawa kani program states the principle plainly: "the value of any recording is only as credible as the person who wrote it down," and its earliest work was determining the most reliable recorders — an explicit rejection of averaging by what is easiest to say or prettiest to translate. The Mutsun dictionary tells its learners directly which attested rival to use ("So use *taprey*"), on evidence about the sources. The tie-breaker ladder above — weighted majority, then attestation density, then conservative retention — is the same discipline made formal.

Cornish's competing revival orthographies were resolved by a negotiated Standard Written Form, agreed in May 2008 after two years of public negotiation through open conferences, published position statements, and an independent commission — and its specification states the principle behind its base-variety choice (it "leans toward a Middle Cornish base, since in many cases the correct… pronunciation can be deduced from an RMC form, but not vice versa"). The community panels of WLRP and the Kaurna foundation gate new and translated material on standing, named review. This project's analogue is the standing linguist-review gate: the candidate laws, the weighting, and both columns of the weight table are submitted, provisional, and gap-marked pending adjudication — and where a genuinely defensible counterfactual exists (equal weights), it is published alongside rather than buried.

## What is claimed, and what stays open

What the derivation does *not* claim: that a distinct Brothertown community language is attested (it is not); that these forms are how the founders spoke (every BL form is generated); or that the weighting is final (it is provisional and adjudication is pending). The decision points named for review are the candidate laws' validity, the weight table's adjudication, the divergent-lexicon selections (which word a given sense-slot should carry), and the morphological expansion path the primer material has only begun to realize.

What the work does claim is narrower and durable: the founding composition and its dialect profile are documented; the shared spine is published comparative history; the daughter-level choices are few, attested on one side or the other, and resolvable under stated rules; the outcome is testable, and its first independent test — the comparison with the modern Mohegan revival — comes back with seven letter-identical rows (six clean anchors plus one kinship-sense note) and a regular-agreement set that traces to the derivation's own recorded patterns. What carries the rest is the discipline already named — marking what is built against what is attested — the very practice the field's most established projects have made standard.
